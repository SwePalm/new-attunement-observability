# Scenario, dependency, 2026-10-02

## 1. Title & Core Question
- Title: Step Six
- Core Question: What does an organisation actually own when all it owns is the intention to leave?

## 2. Context Summary (Translation Layer) – Why This Future Exists
The models that organisations rent have short lives. One lab gives sixty days of notice before retiring a model about a year after release; another has given anywhere from three weeks to six months; a third now publishes two different dates for the same model, depending on which of its doors the customer came through. A provider has also ended access for one named customer after its owner changed. Meanwhile the bodies that could require a tested way out have named the risk and not yet reached the providers: a handful of cloud firms sit inside supervisory lists, while the model makers do not. Lists, inventories and monitoring dashboards are cheap and spread quickly. Taking down a live service on a quiet morning to see whether the alternative works is expensive, and so it is rare. This world is the one where leaving is something everyone has planned and almost no one has done.

## 3. Future World Snapshot (The Lived Experience) – A Day in This Future
Vantage: mid-senior professional inside the institution / a regional insurer's claims intake. Form: a rehearsal, set out as a timed script with the facilitator's margin notes. The Irreplaceable Thing cannot be reached from any lower role, because a planned failure that customers will feel must be signed for by someone who can accept that cost in writing.

T+0:00. Tamsin cuts the claims drafting service's route to the first provider herself, on a morning when nothing is wrong. On the wall by the extinguisher card the old model's expiry date is written in marker. Only the nine people who signed know it is a drill.
Margin: Approval took five weeks. The sentence that won it was "we will make customers wait."

T+0:04. The gateway sends traffic to the second provider. Replies come back, polite and wrong in a new way: they apologise twice and quote a policy clause that does not exist.
Margin: The test set was written against the first model's habits. It passes this one, which proves nothing.

T+0:19. Step six of the runbook says to use the second provider's faster tier. That tier is not offered in the region where the claims data must stay. Two colleagues at adjacent desks give different deadlines for the same model, each reading a different console. "Which console are you on?" Both are right.
Margin: Cross out step six. Do not rewrite it yet.

T+0:41. The second provider's status page says, in full, "Major, resolved." A claims lead reads it aloud like the weather and goes back to retyping a reply by hand. Forty-one customers have waited longer than the hour the firm promises.
Margin: Count them. Someone upstairs will ask.

T+1:12. The director rings. "Is this the real one?" "No, the practice." "Then why is it slow?" "Because it is the first time."

T+1:50. It holds. Not well, but nine people have now found the same twelve errors in the runbook with their own hands, and the claims lead has a corrected prompt in her own words.
Margin: Twelve corrections. Step six now says: ask the vendor, in writing, which region.

T+2:10. Back to the first provider. The route returns as though nothing happened.
Margin: First time anyone here has seen the switch work. The second account's invoice, unopened for eleven months, is paid.

## 4. Behavioral Shifts (Human Lens) – How People Adapt
People learn to read a deprecation notice as a date on a task list rather than as news. The wall calendar of expiry dates becomes an ordinary object in operations rooms, as unremarkable as the fire card. Teams that have run a drill carry something their colleagues lack, a feel for which parts of the system are tied to one model's habits and which are not. The claims lead keeps her own notebook of prompts that survive a change of model, and colleagues from other departments borrow it. The drill is also resented. Frontline staff learn that being told "it is only practice" does not make the waiting customer any less real, and some staff mark the drill hour as the one hour they will not work faster. Elsewhere, organisations that completed the inventory and the monitoring dashboard describe themselves as prepared, and this is believed until the day a notice arrives. Asking "which console are you on?" becomes a courtesy, the way asking someone which line they are on is. Managers learn to ask about cost in hours of customer delay rather than in dollars, since that is the number the director will ask for. Few ask whether the delay should be shared with customers in advance.

## 5. Structural Forces (System Lens) – What Holds This World Together
Model retirement is a schedule set by the provider, with floors that vary by lab: sixty days at one, six months at another, and published floors for the newest models that sit nearly a year out at the first. A customer's continuity therefore depends on which contract it signed and which channel it reads. One provider's two channels now carry two dates for the same models, so even the date is not settled. Ownership is a trigger: a provider can end a named customer's access after a change of control, citing its terms. The supervisory perimeter lags. Lists of critical providers cover cloud hosting and not the model makers, and the guidance that exists, from a prudential regulator, asks for active management of single-provider dependence without prescribing a test. Because statutory clocks run slowly, the pressure that actually moves organisations is contractual and commercial: an insurer's questionnaire, a procurement scorecard, a large customer's audit clause (inference, as the world's habit rather than a present-day fact). The result is a system held together by notices read promptly, second accounts kept alive at low cost, and a few people who have done the drill.

## 6. Reflection & Implications – Questions This World Asks Us
- If a spare is never switched on, is it a spare or a belief?
- Who should bear the cost of a rehearsal that makes real customers wait: the firm that wants to know, the provider whose terms made it necessary, or the customer who did not ask?
- When a date depends on the door you came in through, whose job is it to say which door you are in?

### 7.2 Probable Direction (Near-Term Future) – Where We're Likely Headed
Forced migration is likely to become a yearly operating event, scheduled and resented, as models keep roughly twelve-month lives. Most organisations will respond with the cheap measures: inventories, monitoring, a gateway that makes a second provider reachable. A minority, most likely those with a regulator or a large customer asking, will run live rehearsals. Supervisory change will probably come late. Designations of model providers under the UK or DORA regimes, and any FSB wording on substitutability, are more likely to arrive after the next several model retirements than before them, in keeping with how other announced regulatory dates have slipped. Contractual and commercial pressure, such as notice floors, exit-assistance clauses and insurer questions, is the faster channel and the one to watch (inference). Expect no single event to force the issue: a continuing series of retirements, a few disruptions, and a few customers cut off for reasons of ownership, each too small to prompt a rule.

### 7.3 Preferred Path (Intentional Future) – The Path We Could Choose Instead
- Organisations schedule a live exit rehearsal for each critical model dependency on a calendar, funded as an operating cost.
- Providers publish one date per model across channels, or say plainly which channel it applies to.
- Buyers write notice floors and exit-assistance terms into standard contracts instead of negotiating them after the first notice.
- Supervisors ask regulated firms for evidence that an exit has been exercised, not only that a plan exists.
- Teams keep evaluation sets and prompt notes that follow the work rather than a model identifier.
- Providers publish root-cause accounts for major incidents, so customers can tell a shared cause from a coincidence.

## 8. Connect to Today
### Skills We May Need
- Running a planned failure without panic: designing a drill whose cost is small and known in advance.
- Writing tests that describe the work, not the habits of one model.
- Reading two documents that disagree and deciding what to do while they do.
- Negotiating notice and exit terms before they are needed.
- Counting delay in customers waiting rather than in dollars.
- Keeping a skill alive that is rarely used: a second-provider account in regular, small use.

### Signals & Refractions
- A calendar of model retirement dates in an operations room, kept as plainly as a fire-extinguisher inspection card, is a refraction of the 61-day notice and the Google dates that disagree.
- A status page that says "resolved" without a cause, read at a morning meeting, refracts the incident feed of 29 Sep 2026.
- A team asked to leave a provider because of someone else's purchase refracts the Cursor notice.

## 9. Final Insight
Tamsin turned down, in the spring, a vendor's router that would switch providers by itself when it sensed trouble. The demonstration was convincing and the price small. She asked who would be in the room when it happened, and was told that nobody needed to be. The marker that crossed out step six is now in the claims lead's top drawer, and the next drill is hers to run, though she has not yet said she will.
