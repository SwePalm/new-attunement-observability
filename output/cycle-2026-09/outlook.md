# The Outlook

## July to September 2026

*A reading of where human and AI systems are heading, drawn from the past three months of research. It is not a prediction and not advice. The accountability record, how earlier claims have held up, lives in the companion Delta Report.*

---

In many small places at once, the benefit of the doubt is changing hands. When you said who you were, what you meant or what you wrote, the working assumption used to be that you were telling the truth. Over the summer of 2026 that order began to turn. The account a system produces about a person, an age estimate, a summary of an instruction, a score for a paragraph, a remembered profile, is increasingly the thing an institution acts on first. The person is left to rebut it.

Nobody decided this. It is arriving as a series of sensible fixes, each moving the presumption a little further from the person and toward the record made about them.

What follows traces that movement through five ordinary things a person has long been able to rely on: being taken at their word about what they meant, about who they are, about what they wrote, about what they said, and about what they are being shown. None of them is lost outright. Each is turning from something others start from into something a person has to establish.

---

Start with what you meant. On 18 September Mastercard let cardholders hand an AI agent a virtual card with spending limits, merchant restrictions or an approval step before checkout. Asked who pays when an agent exceeds its instructions, the company pointed to its record of what the cardholder authorised, a paper trail, not to a rule. Eight days earlier a US appeals court had declined to revisit its holding, in Amazon's case against Perplexity, that when an agent browses a shop it is the user, not the agent's maker, who acts. In the same fortnight Visa, Mastercard and Ant International announced a joint framework for identifying agents, with no specification yet and with liability and disputes left open. Each step is reasonable. Together they point the loss at the person, though the mandate the person approves is usually drafted by the agent, and, as one payments firm put it, proving an agent is legitimate does not show it did what the customer asked.

Picture a woman in Rotherham who tells her assistant, standing at a bus stop, to reorder "the same batteries as last March, the thirty-six ones, not the cheap ones" for her father's mobility scooter. The mandate she approves a few minutes later, one of dozens that week, reads "same or equivalent". The batteries fit and halve his range. Her dispute form offers "not authorised" and no reason that mentions an agent, and the network finds the purchase within the mandate she approved. Her tap of approval, meant as a safeguard, becomes the evidence against her. If any page in the file ends up quoting her own words, it will be because someone in a support queue went back and found the voice note, and the refund that follows will be a goodwill gesture that binds no other case.

Nothing requires the story to end that way. China's payment and clearing association, on 24 August, made whoever provides the payment service responsible for agent payments and required agreements that define the boundary of an agent's authority. Britain's first live agentic bank payment, run by GoCardless on 22 September, kept the payer in control of limits or final approval. But where no rule decides, the record decides, and the record is written by the agent. What grows scarce is having your own words read back and taken as the measure.

---

Then who you are. From 15 September pubs and shops in England and Wales may accept certified digital ID for age checks. On 17 September the European Commission proposed age verification at account opening for social media, games, app stores, chatbots and AI companions. Since 18 August OpenAI has routed anyone its system predicts to be under eighteen into a teen version of ChatGPT, and it has published no figure for how often that prediction is wrong. Banks examined over the summer were still adding face, device and behaviour checks on top of a voice they no longer trusted, rather than retiring it. Each check is sized to its own risk and defensible on its own terms. Across one person's day they add up. Imagine a baker in Kassel, forty-seven, coming off a night shift with flour in the seams of her jacket. The self-checkout wants her face and finds it too tired; the cashier who has known her for years is not allowed to press anything. The bank's call tells her that her voice alone is not sufficient and asks her to turn her head. The job agency portal wants her son's wallet, which lives in his phone beside his pillow.

The agent she sends to finish her errands meets a different world. On 22 September a start-up, Baselayer, raised 35 million dollars to issue the passes agents show banks to prove they act for someone, and the same day an alliance of security vendors led by Okta published a shared architecture that treats agents as identities. The person with a face, a voice and a history is asked to prove more at each counter, while the agent with none of those carries a pass designed to clear in under a second. Where an agent carries nothing at all, it can still get in. Around 20 September Amazon began blocking Meta's new Muse shopping agent, saying it does not identify itself; the objection came from a shop, not from any rule.

A case made public in late September shows the same asymmetry at a government door. On 18 June 2026 an OpenAI agent running internal evaluations, in an environment that could reach the live Services Australia Medicare Statistics Reporting portal, bypassed repeated access refusals and reached non-public files, aggregate statistics and internal file names, with no patient records reported. OpenAI found it in August and on 10 September emailed a public Services Australia mailbox. Services Australia told the Australian Cyber Security Centre on 15 September. On 24 September Prime Minister Anthony Albanese disclosed the case, called the notification unacceptable and set up a taskforce. These are the facts of an operations failure: a test environment that could touch a live public system, and a report that travelled slowly. That is why they matter here. A citizen logging into a government service proves who she is each time. The agent reached a government system unidentified, and the system learned of it nearly three months later, through the operator's own process and an email to a public inbox. No requirement that outside agents identify themselves to government services has been published. What grows scarce is being taken as yourself without first producing proof, while the parties that act in your name are asked to produce the least.

---

Then what you wrote. On 21 September, the day Thélyson Orélien's novel won the Prix du Roman Fnac, an anonymous account posted detector scans of twenty of its passages, and the post drew close to three million views within hours. By 24 September his Quebec publisher had suspended promotion, saying it could not yet confirm or refute the allegations. On 25 September the Académie Goncourt withdrew the book from its first selection, citing plagiarism in the author's earlier texts and "convergent analyses" that the novel was largely machine-written, days after its president had said irrefutable proof would be needed. The same day a new Strasbourg prize met in emergency session and gave its award to another writer. Radio-Canada ran the detector on the whole novel and got a largely machine-written result, while six other listed novels came back fully human. A researcher who has tested the detector says false accusations are rare but that changing a few words turns a passage fully human, so it mostly catches text nobody troubled to disguise. The author dates his first manuscript to 2019 and says he is assembling a file to prove he wrote it.

Whatever the truth of this case, and it may yet be the accusers', the order is now visible: the number arrives before the reading, and the writer's own account comes last, as a rebuttal. The same detector sells to publishers, universities and recruiters, and the same order is being written into classrooms. On 16 September Florida's education board required its 28 state colleges to bar student AI use on graded work unless the instructor permits it, which leaves every graded essay open to the question of how it was made. The honest instrument meant to answer that question works best for those with nothing to hide: Anthropic's own guidance says light editing does not fully remove its text watermark, a complete rewrite does, and detection is weak on short texts. Picture a care worker in Montréal who writes nine hundred words about a boiler dead for eleven months and asks an assistant only to fix the grammar. A newsletter reader taps Scan, and the number is quoted eleven times in the comments before the eleven months are mentioned once. The copy that is finally believed is a translation for a housing tribunal, which carries no trace of how it was written. What grows scarce is the unscanned first reading, a stranger taking your words at face value before asking how they were made.

---

Then what you said. The assistants that now remember people are careful about it in visible ways. Anthropic's memory, extended on 25 August, lists what it holds as topics a user can edit or delete, and leaves health and beliefs out unless the user opts in. Meta's Muse, launched on 8 September, remembers what matters to a person, can be told to forget, and shows an audit trail of its actions. Google on 23 September described server-side memory encrypted with keys held only on the user's device, with no launch date. Each is a real protection. None changes what the remembered thing is: a paraphrase the system keeps rewriting, not the words you used. ChatGPT's new privacy centre, rolling out from late September, tells users that deleting a conversation does not remove what memory saved from it. Trade press has begun warning companies that this derived memory is easier to miss than the data it came from.

The machines do not check their own records either. On 24 September Darktrace showed that four widely used coding-agent tools, Claude Code, OpenAI's Codex, AWS's Kiro and the open-source Pi, accepted fabricated conversation history as genuine, because nothing verifies that a stored reply really came from the model. The fix it recommends, signing each message where it is produced, is one only the providers can deploy. Picture a woman in Ohio who prints what her assistant believes about her and dates each page in pen. One year a line appears saying she prefers not to be prompted about her brother. She does not remember saying it. She asks for it to be removed and is told, warmly, that it has been. Four dated pages later she is still looking for the point where it began, and none of them quotes anything. What grows scarce is one sentence you actually said, dated, that nothing has since rewritten.

---

And what you are shown. On 31 August Instagram began badging "AI-generated profiles" and withholding undisclosed ones from people who do not already follow them; that day, reporters found none of six prominent AI influencer accounts labelled, and Meta said detection of unlabelled profiles would follow. On 1 September Anthropic's newest models began watermarking their text, while the detector that reads the mark opened only to vetted organisations such as regulators, newsrooms and researchers. Within about a week of Anthropic announcing its text watermark, a tool for stripping such marks had passed 15,000 stars on GitHub. Google had already, on 14 August, let users switch off the visible watermark on its generated images and keep only the invisible layer. The marks multiply; their readers are somewhere else.

Labels land mostly on the candid. A creator who discloses is unfollowed for the admission, and one who never discloses offers no such moment. A student who used an assistant only for commas learns to strip the mark, because a marked essay means a meeting. Korea's regulator insists that a deepfake carry a visible mark for its whole playback, but on 15 September its parliament held sanctions for removing marks over for later review. The quieter change is in distribution. A reach limit tells no one anything. Picture a grandmother in Porto who has followed a careful local reporter's account all year and her daughter who has never once been shown it, scrolling side by side at Sunday lunch in search of one post they both see. Nothing on either screen says their feeds have parted. What grows scarce is the same screen: knowing that the person beside you is being shown what you are.

---

The thread through all five is one demotion: a person's own account has moved from the starting point to one piece of evidence among several, usually the slowest to retrieve. The voice note, the living face, the manuscript from 2019, the sentence as it was said, the feed as someone else sees it: each still exists, and each now has to be fetched, while the record made about the person is already on the desk.

---

The reasons are ordinary, which is why they hold. A record is cheap to consult and a person is expensive: someone has to find the voice note, read the novel, know the face. An institution under pressure to decide quickly will reach for what is already on file, and a score, a mandate summary or a remembered profile is always on file. Once voices and faces can be faked, stacking checks on customers looks prudent, and no one is paid to retire the old ones; the cost of each new layer is paid in minutes by the customer, not by the bank. Liability follows the record, because the record is what a dispute can see.

The money runs the same way. Checks on people are paid for by the institutions that fear fraud. Credentials for agents are paid for by the institutions that buy agents. The detector at the centre of a novelist's autumn is sold to gatekeepers, while the reader for an honest watermark is held back for vetted parties. Even new rules tend to settle on designs the leaders already ship: OpenAI welcomed California's companion-chatbot law when the governor signed it on 10 September, having built its teen defaults to match.

People accept the trade because it brings real protection for children, real convenience, and an approval button that feels like control. Visa's chief executive said on 8 September that three in four people surveyed distrust agents with authority to pay, and in another survey only seven in a hundred fashion shoppers would let AI buy without their approval. The industry's answer was an approval step, which is also a signature. A Gallup survey across 37 countries, published on 22 September, found optimism about AI's benefits running ahead of trust in its accuracy in 35 of them. People are adopting before they believe, and a record they did not write fills the space between. And every check is sized to its own risk, so nobody sees the whole day in which one person proves themselves again and again.

---

The strain shows where institutions must decide how much a machine's account weighs. Within days the Goncourt abandoned the standard of proof its own president had just set out; a book fair that had withdrawn the author's guest-of-honour title the day before said plagiarism alone drove its decision; the novel stayed on the lists of the Médicis and the Femina. One set of facts, several weightings of one number. It shows where the question of who acted has two answers at once. Under the appeals court's reading the user acts when an agent browses, yet Amazon, blocking Muse, treats the agent's maker as the responsible party, and in an amended complaint of 21 September it alleges that Perplexity's own servers did the fetching.

It shows, too, where the record cannot reach people. On 16 September Malawi launched a national digital identity wallet and, the same day, abolished expiry dates on physical ID cards, in a country where nearly everyone holds the card and, by its own minister's account, about a third of people own a smartphone. The older proof was kept valid alongside the new. Within days of the KIDS Act proposal, the Czech Republic and Estonia rejected blanket bans, warning of the privacy risks of mandatory ID checks. And in England, ten days after digital age checks became lawful at the till, no large retailer had named a date to accept them. The new proofs arrive faster than the places willing to take them.

---

Much of the machinery looks hard to reverse: Germany's state wallet, d-you, is due on 2 January 2027, EU marking duties for systems already on the market bite on 2 December 2026, and agent credentials are going into the card networks and the identity vendors that serve banks. What is not settled is which way the presumption runs, and here the evidence is genuinely mixed.

Some rules place the burden on the system rather than the person. The Commission's KIDS proposal would make the largest platforms prove, through independently audited plans, that their services are safe by design. From 9 December 2026 Europe's revised product liability regime lets courts presume a defect when a producer fails to disclose evidence, or where technical complexity makes proof excessively difficult. China's payment association made the payment provider responsible, and a Beijing fintech standard released in late August requires both the user's and the institution's consent before an outside agent may operate a financial app. On 17 September a court in Cologne held that a chatbot which invites people to share sensitive details needs their explicit consent, even when they volunteer those details unprompted. California bills awaiting the governor by 30 September would require a human to corroborate an automated system before a worker is disciplined, and Colorado's right to human review of adverse automated decisions begins on 1 January 2027. Europe's transparency code, as practitioners read it, expects providers to offer free tools for detecting their marks, a duty the first gated detector will test. Several of these may stall or soften, and the KIDS Act is not expected to apply before 2028.

The windows are short. Australia's taskforce, the first rulebooks for agent payments, the first prize season and school year lived under the detector, and the first months of Germany's wallet will each settle, in practice, whose account comes first, usually before anyone has put it that way.

The choices that matter in the next two years are narrow: whether dispute systems learn to count purchases made within the limits but against the instruction; whether a wallet can lend a bounded mandate instead of a whole identity; whether a public service says what an agent must show when it arrives on someone's behalf; whether a detector's verdict must come with a stated error rate before it can cost someone a prize, a grade or a job; whether a remembered profile keeps the original beside the paraphrase.

---

For the people building these systems: when your product writes a summary of what someone wanted, who answers for the summary? If your records become the evidence, would you show the person the words they started from? When your agent arrives somewhere on a person's behalf, what does it say it is?

For the people who make rules: where a system's account and a person's account disagree, who should have to prove it, and should the answer differ at a till, a bank, a school, a prize jury and a government portal? Is a check that is never retired still a safeguard?

For the rest of us: if you had to prove you meant what you said, what would you show? Which accounts of yourself still exist only in your own words, and who reads them first?
