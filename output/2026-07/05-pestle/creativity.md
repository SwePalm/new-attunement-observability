### Political
Core Shift Thesis:
Creative-rights policy is being made by courts, not legislatures, and the judiciary has settled the core questions in ways that favor training over individual compensation.
Forces:
1. The Supreme Court's Thaler cert denial (Mar 2026) constitutionalizes the human-authorship requirement, making it the fixed reference point for all creative-AI policy.
2. Judicial fair-use holdings (Bartz, Kadrey) set training policy without legislative debate, leaving elected bodies as spectators to a settled architecture.
3. EU watermarking/provenance deferral (to Dec 2026) delays the one mechanism that would let policy distinguish human from synthetic creative output.
4. Guild bargaining (SAG-AFTRA, Writers Guild AI terms) becomes the political channel through which organized creators exert influence absent legislation.

### Economic
Core Shift Thesis:
Creative value flows by leverage: franchise-scale IP monetizes AI participation, platforms internalize production, and the individual-creator middle is structurally excluded from the training economy.
Forces:
1. Disney–OpenAI $1B/3-year licensing of 200+ characters demonstrates franchise-scale IP is a monetizable AI asset, leverage converts to revenue.
2. Bartz's holding (training is fair use, no licensing entitlement) removes the individual creator's royalty hook while the $1.5B settlement rewards only litigable piracy.
3. Thomson Reuters v. Ross locates liability at market substitution, protecting incumbents' direct-competition markets but not creators' training contributions.
4. Zero-marginal-cost generation in known styles erodes the scarcity premium that sustained working-creator incomes.

### Social
Core Shift Thesis:
The creative class bifurcates along a now-legal line between owners of leverage and owners of only work, narrowing who can afford a creative career.
Forces:
1. Franchise owners and platforms capture rising returns while working creators face falling returns to individual creative labor, redistribution, not destruction.
2. Uncompensated-contribution-made-visible (creators seeing their style in outputs, lawfully) produces a distinct social injury with no legal remedy.
3. Defensive creator behavior (withholding, data poisoning, paywalling) rises as value-denial substitutes for unavailable value-capture.
4. Cultural production drifts toward the already-capitalized, threatening the diversity of who gets to be a working artist.

### Technological
Core Shift Thesis:
Generative platforms are becoming creative infrastructure, and provenance tooling becomes the contested boundary between human and synthetic authorship.
Forces:
1. Franchise IP flowing into generative video (Sora) makes platforms the distribution and production layer for licensed creative worlds.
2. Provenance/watermarking (SynthID at 10B+ items) is the technical substrate for any future human-authorship or licensing distinction, but adoption is voluntary where the EU mandate slipped.
3. Style-level generation blurs the authorship line the law requires (Thaler), making the human/AI-blend boundary a technical detection problem.
4. Persistent creator-style models raise the prospect of identifiable per-creator training footprints, the technical precondition for any collective licensing market.

### Legal
Core Shift Thesis:
The core creative-AI legal architecture is largely settled, with a conspicuous vacuum where individual-creator compensation would sit.
Forces:
1. Training = fair use (Bartz, Kadrey); acquisition-by-piracy = liable (Bartz); AI-only output = uncopyrightable (Thaler): a coherent, settled rule set.
2. The substitution boundary (Thomson Reuters v. Ross) governs where fair use ends, protecting direct-competition markets only.
3. No collective-licensing institution exists for individual training rights, the legal hook (a royalty obligation) was removed by Bartz.
4. Guild AI-terms bargaining extends the labor-contract model into creative-rights territory, filling part of the vacuum for organized creators only.

### Environmental
Core Shift Thesis:
Generative creative production shifts cultural output onto the same energy-intensive compute substrate, coupling culture-making to infrastructure concentration.
Forces:
1. High-volume generative media (video especially, e.g. Sora) is compute-intensive, tying creative production to the ~$700B datacenter buildout.
2. Cultural production concentrating on a few platforms couples the diversity of culture to the concentration of infrastructure.
3. The marginal environmental cost of synthetic content approaches zero per item at scale, incentivizing volume over scarcity in ways that reshape creative economics and resource use.
