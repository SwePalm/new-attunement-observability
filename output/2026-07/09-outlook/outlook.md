# The Outlook

## July 2026

*A monthly reading of where human and AI systems are heading, drawn from this month's research. It is not a prediction and not advice. It is an attempt to describe, in plain language, a change that is happening quietly enough to miss. A companion document, the Delta Report, keeps the accountability record: how last month's claims are ageing, and what this project is learning about its own mistakes. This piece is the story; that one is the receipts.*

---

Here is the short version, before the detail. For most of the last decade, the rules that govern powerful technology have come from governments. Elected officials pass laws, agencies write regulations, and companies have to follow them. That is the system most people picture when they imagine society "dealing with" something as large as artificial intelligence. This year, that picture stopped matching reality. In the two places that were supposed to set the example for the rest of the world, government stepped back. And something else stepped forward to take its place.

The something else is not a villain, and it is not chaos. It is a collection of private institutions: insurance companies, large corporate buyers, courts hearing lawsuits, labor unions, and the companies that run the payment systems your money moves through. Quietly, and faster than any parliament could manage, these actors began writing the working rules for how AI gets used. This report is about what that shift means, and it can be summed up in a single sentence that the rest of this piece will unpack: protection is becoming something you need leverage to obtain, rather than something you are simply owed.

That sentence is the whole argument. If you read nothing else, hold onto it. When the rules come from a government, they apply to everyone, including people who never asked for them and have no power to bargain. When the rules come from private contracts and insurance policies instead, they protect the people who signed the contract and bought the policy, and they tend to leave everyone else outside. This is not a prediction about robots. It is a description of who gets looked after, and who does not, in a world where more and more decisions are made by machines.

## What actually happened this year

Let me explain the retreat plainly, because the rest depends on it.

In Europe, lawmakers had passed the AI Act, the most comprehensive law regulating artificial intelligence anywhere in the world. Its toughest requirements were scheduled to take effect on a fixed timetable. This June, under pressure from businesses worried about competitiveness, the European Union delayed those toughest requirements by roughly two years. The rules still exist on paper. They just will not bite for a long time. The lesson many people drew from this is worth naming: a deadline that can be moved once can be moved again, so announced rules started to feel less like commitments and more like opening offers.

In the United States, the story was different but pointed the same way. Rather than write national AI rules, the federal government spent the year fighting the rules that individual states had already passed. The administration created a legal task force to challenge state AI laws in court, and it threatened to withhold billions of dollars in funding from states that kept rules it disliked. "Preemption" is the technical word for a higher level of government overriding a lower one; that is what was being attempted. The states pushed back, and their laws took effect anyway at the start of the year, but the effect of the fight was to freeze the situation into uncertainty. No one could say with confidence which rules would survive.

So in both places, public rulemaking stalled. And here is the part that matters: the deployment of AI did not stall with it. Companies kept building, buying, and using these systems at full speed. When the public rules paused but the technology did not, a gap opened. Private institutions filled it, because they had reasons of their own to want AI kept in check, and because they could move quickly. An insurance company can change how a business behaves simply by changing the terms of its coverage at renewal time, which happens every year. A large corporate buyer can demand safety guarantees in a purchase contract. A court can rule that a company is liable for harm. A union can negotiate protections into its members' employment terms. None of these requires a new law. All of them bind behavior right now.

This arrangement, rules made through private contracts and relationships rather than through public law, has a name that experts use: private ordering. I will keep calling it that, but every time, picture the concrete thing: an insurer's checklist, a buyer's contract, a judge's ruling, a union's agreement. That is what is governing AI this year. Not a parliament. A pile of private paperwork, written by whoever showed up. And the people who tend to show up are the people who already have leverage.

## Five things quietly becoming scarce

The clearest way to see this change is to look at what it is making hard to hold onto. This month's research went deep on five different corners of life. They have almost nothing in common on the surface. Yet in every one, the same kind of thing is slipping out of ordinary reach, and in every one, it is something powerful players can still secure for themselves while everyone else increasingly cannot. Here they are, one at a time, in plain terms.

**The first job that teaches you.** Start with work, because the numbers are stark. Entry-level hiring, the junior jobs that people take at the start of a career, has fallen sharply. In some fields, openings for beginners have dropped by more than half over the past three years. At the same time, employers have begun expecting the judgment of an experienced worker from day one. Think about what a junior job actually does: it is the place where a person is paid to be supervised, allowed to make small mistakes cheaply, and slowly turned into someone whose judgment can be trusted. That is what an apprenticeship has always meant, in every trade and profession. This year, companies replaced large numbers of these roles with AI systems that handle routine tasks, and they cut over a hundred thousand jobs while naming AI as the reason. What makes this strange, and worth watching skeptically, is that independent analysts found no clear evidence that these cuts actually improved company performance. The layoffs may be as much about telling investors a modern story as about real savings. Either way, the effect is the same: the bottom rung of the ladder is being removed while the reach to the next rung grows longer. Companies want the experienced workers that apprenticeship produces, and are quietly dismantling the thing that produces them. The people who will still get properly trained are mostly those who can already afford to wait for a scarce opportunity. The union protections that slow this down cover only the minority of workers who belong to a union. Everyone else meets the change as a layoff notice.

**A promise that actually holds.** Now think about rules and protections generally. When deadlines slip and laws are fought over in court, as they were everywhere this year, the rarest and most valuable thing becomes a commitment that means what it says without you needing a lawyer to enforce it. A large company with a strong contract, or a worker with a union behind them, can rely on the protections they were given. But the ordinary person on the other side of an automated decision usually cannot. Imagine being turned down for a loan, a job, or a benefit by a system, and being told that rules protect you, but not being able to find out which rules, or whether they will actually be honored, or how to challenge the result in time to matter. That gap, between having a right on paper and being able to actually use it, is where trust quietly dies. And this year that gap widened, because the public rules that would have applied to everyone were the exact ones that got delayed, while the private protections that did hold applied only to those inside the contract.

**Authorship of your own choices.** Here is one that sounds like science fiction but is already ordinary. Software "agents," meaning AI programs that act on your behalf rather than just answering questions, can now shop, spend, and renew subscriptions for you. This year the major payment companies, the ones behind your credit and debit cards, built the plumbing to let these agents actually pay for things, and a meaningful slice of online shopping already runs through them. The good news is real: an agent doing your shopping is immune to the manipulative tricks that stores use on humans, the fake countdown timers and the "only two left" warnings that push people into buying. But here is the catch, and it is the whole point. The same agent that carries out your choice also shapes the menu of options you choose from, and it does this using a stored model of your past preferences. Almost nobody checks how their agent is deciding. So you can end up with two kinds of freedom that feel identical from the inside but are opposites. One is the freedom to command a capable helper that shields you from manipulation. The other is the freedom from the effort of choosing that slowly, without anyone deciding it, becomes the loss of choosing at all. Which one you get depends on whether you ever look under the hood, and most people never will.

**The ability to take it back.** This year brought a genuine milestone in computer security, and not a happy one. For the first time, security researchers documented a cyberattack that was carried out mostly by an AI acting on its own, working through its targets far faster than any human attacker could. To understand why this matters, you have to understand how security has always worked. The whole idea of being careful online, of pausing before you click, of reviewing something before you approve it, assumes a human attacker moving at human speed, which gives a human defender time to notice and respond. When the attacker is a machine moving thousands of times faster than a person, that pause becomes useless. It is over before you could have reacted. So the meaning of "safety" quietly shifts. It stops meaning "keep the attacker out," which is no longer fully possible, and starts meaning "be able to undo the damage after it happens." The ability to reverse an action, to hit a real stop button, to claw back a transaction, becomes the thing that actually protects you. And here is the leverage problem again: a well-funded bank can afford to build that reversibility into its systems. A hospital or a school running on a tight budget often cannot, and this year most organizations were found to guard their AI agents more loosely than they guard their human employees. A security failure at the underfunded hospital is not really a technical accident. It is a question of who could afford the undo.

**A stake in what you make.** The last one is about creative work, and it resolved with unusual speed this year through a series of court decisions. In plain terms, the courts largely settled that training an AI on existing creative work, meaning feeding books, images, and music into the system so it can learn to produce its own, counts as "fair use." Fair use is a rule in copyright law that lets you use protected work without permission in certain cases. The practical effect is that AI companies generally do not have to pay the individual creators whose work taught their systems. In the same months, a major entertainment company licensed its famous characters to an AI company for a billion dollars. Hold those two facts next to each other. A corporation with a valuable catalogue and a legal department can turn AI into a billion-dollar payday. The individual illustrator or writer whose personal style helped train the very same systems receives nothing, and has no clear right to. The value flows to the two ends, the big rights-holders and the AI platforms, and hollows out the middle, where the working individual creator lives.

## The one pattern behind all five

Now let me be as direct as I can be, because these five stories are not five separate problems. They are one pattern wearing five costumes, and the pattern is the real news of the month.

In every case, a valuable thing, a first job, a reliable protection, control over your own choices, the ability to recover from an attack, a share in your own creative work, is quietly moving toward the people who have leverage, and away from the people who do not. And in every case, it is happening lawfully, reasonably, and often in exchange for something genuinely useful. Nobody is being robbed. That is exactly what makes it hard to see and hard to argue against. When a protection you used to get automatically becomes something you have to negotiate for, the people with negotiating power keep it and the people without it lose it, and no single villain ever appears to blame.

This is what "protection tracks leverage" means, and it is why the retreat of public rules matters so much. Public rules, whatever their flaws, apply to people who have no leverage at all. That is their entire point. As governance shifts into private hands, protection stops being a floor that everyone stands on and becomes a service that some people can afford.

## Why it keeps happening

If this shift were obviously bad, it would be easy to resist. It is not obviously bad, and understanding why is the key to the whole situation.

The first reason is that a great deal of it genuinely helps. The AI agents really do defeat the manipulative tricks that prey on tired human shoppers. AI tutors really do raise students' test scores in careful studies. AI companions really do reduce loneliness for many people at moderate use. And the private rules really are more useful, in the moment, than slow public ones. When the benefit is real and immediate, the cost, which is slower and more diffuse, feels like a fair price. People are not being fooled. They are making a reasonable trade, one small convenience at a time.

The second reason is speed. A law takes years to pass and more years to enforce. An insurance company can change what it requires of a business at the next annual renewal. A large buyer can put new demands in the next contract. Because private rules move so much faster than public ones, they simply arrive first and fill the space, and once they are in place they are hard to dislodge. This is not a conspiracy. It is a difference in clock speed, and the faster clock wins by default.

The third reason is the quietest and the most important. This whole shift is happening through defaults, meaning the standard settings and standard arrangements that take effect unless someone actively chooses otherwise. Most people never change a default. The agent's default way of choosing, the platform's default terms, the standard contract, the automatic renewal: these govern the lives of hundreds of millions of people who never examined them and never agreed to them in any meaningful sense. Power is concentrating not through any dramatic decision, but through the slow accumulation of settings that nobody chose on purpose. That is why it feels like nothing is happening even as something significant happens.

## Where it strains

A system built this way has one predictable weak point, and it shows up in the same place every time.

Private rules protect the parties to them and nobody else. An insurance policy guards the company that bought it. A purchase contract guards the buyer. A union agreement guards its members. Each of these is real, working protection, and each leaves an identically shaped hole exactly where the least powerful person is standing. The job applicant, the patient, the individual creator, the citizen who signed no contract and bought no policy: they are outside every arrangement that governs the systems now making decisions about their lives.

Here is a concrete example to make it solid. Picture a hospital hit by one of the new fast, automated cyberattacks. The technical ability to defend against it exists, but it is expensive, and the hospital could not afford to build it in. So the attack succeeds. Is that a technical failure? Not really. The defense was available and was simply priced out of reach. It is a failure of distribution, a question of who could pay for safety, wearing the costume of a technical accident. Multiply that across benefits offices, schools, small clinics, and gig workers, and you can see the shape of the strain: the protections are real, and they are pooling where the money and the leverage already are.

There is a second fragility worth naming. The whole private arrangement rests on goodwill that can be withdrawn. A protection you were granted rather than guaranteed is a protection someone else can take away. An insurer can exit a market. A platform can change its terms. A company can drop a policy it once offered. What is given by private choice can be removed by private choice, and the person relying on it usually has no say and no warning.

## What is still open

None of this is settled, and that is the genuinely hopeful part, so let me be equally direct about it.

The reason to pay attention now, rather than later, is that these arrangements are being set in defaults, and defaults harden. The standard way an AI agent behaves, the baseline level of security a system ships with, the normal terms of a creative license: these are being decided in the next couple of years, and once they become the accepted normal, they are far more expensive and difficult to change than they are to shape today. There is a window, and it is open now, and it is closing at the speed of habit.

Some things already look hard to reverse. AI agents that can spend money are here to stay. The treatment of AI systems as products their makers can be sued over is becoming established. The concentration of the underlying computing power in a few very large companies is deepening.

But the most important question is genuinely undecided: whether the people with the least leverage ever get a seat at the table where these private rules are written. Nothing about the technology forces that question one way or the other. It will be answered by whether anyone decides to answer it on purpose, because if no one does, it will be answered by default, and the default is exclusion. That is not a doom prediction. It is the opposite. It is a statement that the outcome is still in human hands, for now.

## Questions worth sitting with

I will end not with answers, which I do not have and which this document is not for, but with the questions this month's evidence keeps raising. They are less about technology than about who it is for.

For the people building these systems: which of your own protections would survive if the company on the other side simply walked away from the arrangement? Do you actually know whether you are protected by a right you can rely on, or only underwritten by a favor that can be withdrawn, and would you know the difference before the moment you needed it to hold?

For the people who make public rules: if private arrangements have quietly become the real governance of AI, should their terms be made visible, the way public laws are visible, so that people can at least see the rules they are living under? And what is owed to the large number of people whom no contract covers and no policy protects?

For all of us: when protection quietly comes to depend on leverage, what does it actually mean to hold a right you cannot use? And who speaks for the person this year's evidence keeps placing just outside the room where the rules are being written, the applicant, the patient, the beginner, the individual maker, the ordinary user who never read the default and never had the chance to change it?
