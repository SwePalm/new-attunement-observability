## 1. Title & Core Question
- Title: The Interregnum
- Core Question: When public AI rules retreat and private ordering takes the load, who is actually protected — and can a society tell the difference between being governed and being underwritten?

## 2. Context Summary (Translation Layer) – Why This Future Exists
By the late 2020s, the two great Western attempts to govern AI had both blinked: the EU deferred its landmark obligations under competitiveness pressure, and the US federal government spent its energy suppressing state rules rather than writing national ones. AI deployment never paused. Into the vacuum moved the institutions that could bind behavior without legislatures: insurers made AI coverage conditional on logging and human oversight; large buyers wrote governance into procurement contracts; courts extended product liability through the AI supply chain; unions bargained deployment rules for their members. The result is a functioning but unequal governance system — dense protection inside commercial and organized relationships, thin air outside them. Companies comply constantly, with counterparties rather than with law. Citizens navigate AI-mediated decisions with rights that depend on contracts they have never seen.

## 3. Future World Snapshot (The Lived Experience) – A Day in This Future
Amara runs "obligations engineering" at a mid-sized European HR-tech firm — a title that did not exist three years ago. Her Tuesday begins with the deadline dashboard: the statutory obligations pane shows the twice-deferred high-risk rules in their familiar amber ("assumed +12 months, confidence 40%"), while the binding pane — insurer conditions, customer audit rights, the works council's AI clause — glows a serious red with real dates. Nobody at the firm remembers finding this inversion strange.

At 10:00 her insurer's quarterly conformance review starts. The adjuster, not any regulator, walks through incident logs and asks whether the new candidate-ranking feature keeps a human decision point. Coverage renewal — and therefore the product launch — turns on her answers. She has prepared for this meeting the way her predecessors prepared for government inspections that, in her sector, never came.

Midday brings the harder case. A rejected job applicant has written asking under which rules his screening was conducted. The honest answer is a lattice: the customer's procurement terms require explainability summaries, the insurer requires decision traces, no statute currently requires anything. Amara drafts the reply she always drafts — accurate, kind, and useless to him — and flags the case to legal, who note that a product-liability suit could reach even their upstream model provider, so the file must be immaculate.

At day's end she updates the "promise register", her own invention: a list of commitments the firm has made that it will honor even if never forced to — one appeal path, one notice period, one audit format frozen for two years. Customers have started citing the register in deals. It is the only page on the dashboard that has never slipped.

## 4. Behavioral Shifts (Human Lens) – How People Adapt
People learn to ask "who backs this?" instead of "is this legal?" — provenance of protection replaces legality as the practical question. Job applicants and benefits claimants develop folk knowledge of which employers and agencies have contestable processes, sharing recourse maps the way earlier generations shared salary information. Workers gravitate toward unionized or well-insured employers not for wages but for the enforceable notice-and-appeal rights that have become the visible marker of a governed workplace.

Professionals convert: compliance officers become obligations engineers fluent in contract stacks; a small industry of "commitment auditors" verifies whether firms' voluntary promises are real. And a quiet consumer instinct emerges — loyalty to any institution whose promises demonstrably hold, because such institutions have become rare enough to be remarkable.

## 5. Structural Forces (System Lens) – What Holds This World Together
Three forces stabilize the interregnum. First, insurance conditionality: after AI exclusions became the default in standard liability policies, the specialty insurers who filled the gap acquired regulator-like power — their logging, oversight, and containment conditions are enforced quarterly, at renewal, with perfect attendance. Second, judicial deterrence: product-liability doctrine that classifies consumer AI as products and reaches upstream to model providers makes every layer of the supply chain price harms it cannot fully observe, disciplining even firms that face no statute. Third, procurement scale: large buyers' governance terms cascade down supply chains, standardizing controls the way no legislature managed. What the system lacks is coverage: these forces protect counterparties, not bystanders — the applicant, the end user, the laid-off worker stand outside every contract that governs the systems deciding their lives.

## 6. Reflection & Implications – Questions This World Asks Us
If protection flows through contracts, is a person without bargaining power still a citizen of this system, or its weather?
Does governance-by-insurance select for auditable harms over important ones — optimizing what can be logged rather than what matters?
When the deferred statutes finally arrive, will they codify the private norms already in place — and if so, who elected those norms?

## 7. Pullback Layer: From Possibility to Probability
### 7.1 Signals Emerging (Plausible Zone) – Early Signals We Already See
- The EU's June 2026 omnibus deferred Annex III/I high-risk obligations to Dec 2027/Aug 2028 while transparency rules held for Aug 2026.
- The US executive order created a DOJ task force litigating state AI laws and conditioned $42B in broadband funds on their repeal; state laws took effect anyway in January 2026.
- ISO made AI exclusions the default in commercial general liability while affirmative AI insurance (Armilla, Testudo) launched to sell the gap back.
- Courts classified a consumer AI app as a product and reached the upstream model provider via component-part liability; the cases settled with committed safety features (Jan 2026).
- Union contracts (NewsGuild's 58 agreements, Culinary Union, Microsoft units) now carry enforceable AI notice-and-bargain provisions.
- 77% of organizations report building AI governance programs, driven by procurement and insurer demands rather than statute.

### 7.2 Probable Direction (Near-Term Future) – Where We're Likely Headed
The likeliest path through 2030 is a maturing interregnum rather than a resolution: the deferred EU obligations arrive late but arrive, codifying practices the insurance-procurement complex has already standardized; US federalism litigation produces mixed precedents that freeze the state-federal balance without settling it. Private governance densifies — shared audit formats, common incident taxonomies — because interoperability cuts costs for insurers and buyers alike. Protection inequality persists as the system's signature: measurable, discussed, and unresolved, because those outside the contract lattice have no mechanism that converts their exposure into rules. The calibration lesson (CAL-002) applies to this scenario itself: announced re-regulation should be discounted; the private regime has at least a three-to-five-year run.

### 7.3 Preferred Path (Intentional Future) – The Path We Could Choose Instead
- Enforce something on schedule: honoring the Aug 2026 transparency obligations against a named deployer would restore the credibility of public deadlines at minimal cost.
- Mandate publication of AI-relevant insurance and procurement conditions in consequential domains, converting private norms into public reference points.
- Extend recourse beyond contract: statutory minimum appeal rights for consequential AI decisions, enforceable regardless of vendor relationships.
- Tie any federal preemption to a federal floor — no displacement of state protections without replacement protections.
- Fund collective-bargaining and civil-society capacity as governance infrastructure, since they are currently the only mechanisms writing rules for individuals.

## 8. Connect to Today
### Skills We May Need
- Reading a contract stack the way one reads a statute: tracing which clause actually protects whom.
- Designing "promise registers" — auditable voluntary commitments that hold without enforcement.
- Negotiating AI clauses in employment contexts before deployment, not after.
- Translating insurance conditions into engineering requirements and back.
- Distinguishing being governed from being underwritten, in institutions one joins or builds.

### Signals & Refractions
- An insurer's conformance review already carries more operational weight than a regulator's guidance letter in most AI-deploying firms.
- "Which rules covered this decision?" is becoming a question institutions cannot answer in one sentence — the complexity is itself the signal.
- The promise register has a present-day seed: firms publishing voluntary model-behavior commitments and being publicly graded on adherence.

## 9. Final Insight
The interregnum is not lawlessness; it is law by other means, written by whoever shows up. Insurers, buyers, judges, and unions showed up. The open question of the next few years is not whether AI will be governed — it demonstrably is — but whether the people with the most at stake and the least leverage will ever be parties to the governance, or only subjects of it. A society that cannot tell being governed from being underwritten will eventually discover the difference at the worst possible moment: when it needs a promise to hold and finds only a policy exclusion.
