Turn 1 – Conceptual Frame

Governance has always rested on a quiet assumption: that announced rules eventually become enforced rules. The first half of 2026 broke that assumption twice, in opposite directions, in the two jurisdictions that were supposed to define the global template. The EU, having built the world's most comprehensive AI statute, deferred its core high-risk obligations by sixteen to twenty-four months under competitiveness pressure — Parliament endorsed the Digital Omnibus on June 16, the Council approved it on June 29. The US federal executive, rather than writing rules of its own, turned its energy against the only operative American AI rules: a December 2025 executive order created a DOJ litigation task force against state AI laws and conditioned $42 billion in broadband funds on their repeal. The conceptual novelty is not deregulation — that is old — but the emergence of what might be called negative governance: the active use of state power to prevent governance from occurring anywhere else. What fills the space is not nothing. Procurement contracts now demand audit rights and incident reporting as routine vendor terms. ISO made AI exclusions the default in commercial liability policies, forcing a specialty market of affirmative AI insurance into existence. Courts classified a conversational AI system as a product, with strict liability attached. Union contracts wrote notice-and-bargain obligations for AI deployment. Each of these is binding in the only sense that matters operationally: violating them costs money now, not after a deferred deadline. The conceptual frame for the next two years is therefore an inversion of the usual regulatory story. Public law is becoming aspirational while private ordering becomes compulsory. Attunement between humans and AI systems is being governed — but by counterparties, not by citizens.

Turn 2 – Societal Reframing

Societally, the migration of governance into private ordering redistributes protection along the lines of bargaining power. A Fortune 500 buyer can demand model documentation, audit rights, and indemnification from an AI vendor; a gig worker or benefits applicant subject to that same vendor's system can demand nothing. A unionized newsroom gets contractual notice before AI restructures its work — 58 NewsGuild contracts now carry such clauses — while the non-unionized majority learns about restructuring from a layoff notice. The 123,000 tech job cuts of H1 2026, with AI the most-cited reason, landed almost entirely outside any governance framework at all. This is the societal meaning of the omnibus deferral that statistics conceal: statutory governance is the only kind that applies equally to people who did not negotiate for it. When the EU moved recruitment and employment AI obligations to December 2027, it did not pause the deployment of those systems; it paused the only rules that would have covered the people those systems evaluate. Society is thus running a natural experiment: two more years of AI diffusion governed by whoever happens to hold contractual leverage. Public trust data suggests people sense this asymmetry — the 2026 Edelman Barometer found only 34% comfortable with government use of AI, and 54% of low-income respondents expecting AI to leave them behind. The social contract question underneath the legal one is stark: whether legitimacy can survive a period in which the loudest public promises about AI safety are the ones being formally postponed.

Turn 3 – Psychological Implications

The psychology of deferred governance operates through expectation management. A deadline that slips once recalibrates every subsequent deadline; the omnibus taught every compliance officer, vendor, and citizen that AI-rule dates are opening bids, not commitments. This produces a specific cognitive stance — call it regulatory fatalism — in which actors discount announced rules and attend only to enforced ones. The stance is individually rational and collectively corrosive: it rewards firms that bet against enforcement and embarrasses those that invested in early compliance, teaching the market that diligence is a competitive disadvantage. Inside organizations, the psychological burden lands on the governance functions that spent 2025 building EU-readiness programs. Their budgets survive — insurers and procurement still demand controls — but their narrative changed from "the law requires this" to "our counterparties require this," a weaker story that reshapes internal authority. For individuals subject to AI decisions, the psychology is one of recourse uncertainty: the person denied a loan or screened out of a hiring pipeline in 2026 cannot know which rules, if any, protected them, because the answer depends on an invisible lattice of contracts and insurance terms. Uncertainty about protection is itself a harm; people who cannot tell whether they have recourse behave as if they have none — they do not contest, do not appeal, do not trust. The Edelman finding that experience with AI increases trust cuts both ways: experience of unaccountable AI decisions compounds distrust in exactly the populations already most skeptical.

Turn 4 – Institutional Dynamics

Institutionally, four systems are absorbing the load that legislatures shed, each with different capacities and blind spots. Courts move first and slowest: the Character.AI product-liability ruling and its component-part extension to Google establish doctrine with real deterrent force, but case-by-case, years after harm, and only where plaintiffs can litigate. Insurers move fastest: ISO's default AI exclusions and the Armilla/Testudo affirmative products reprice AI risk quarterly, effectively legislating logging, human oversight, and bounded autonomy as conditions of coverage — but insurers protect balance sheets, not rights, and uninsurable practices simply migrate to firms willing to run bare. Procurement governs at scale — 77% of organizations building governance programs cite buyer requirements — but it governs vendors, not outcomes, and its standards are trade secrets rather than public norms. Unions govern deepest but narrowest, writing enforceable deployment rules for the roughly one-in-ten workers they represent. The dynamic among these four is largely uncoordinated, and the federal-state conflict adds active interference: state laws that took effect in January (California's frontier transparency act, Texas's governance act) face DOJ litigation and funding blackmail, meaning even the operative public rules carry litigation discounts. The institutional question for the next 18 months is whether these private systems converge on interoperable norms — shared evidence formats, common incident taxonomies — or fragment into contractual dialects that make cross-institutional accountability practically impossible.

Turn 5 – Long-Term Trajectory

Projected to 2030, two trajectories bracket the plausible space. In the convergence path, private ordering matures into scaffolding for eventual public law: insurance conditions, procurement standards, and court doctrine harmonize through repetition; the deferred EU obligations arrive in 2027–2028 and codify what the market already practices; a US Congress eventually legislates atop the same norms. Private governance functions as regulation's rough draft — historically common, from fire codes to accounting standards. In the divergence path, the interregnum becomes the settlement: deferred deadlines slip again (the ledger now carries a claim testing whether Dec 2027 holds), preemption litigation freezes state innovation without federal replacement, and the norms that consolidate are those profitable to insurers and buyers — auditability yes, individual recourse no. Governance becomes a property of commercial relationships, and protection becomes a feature you purchase. The observable difference between paths within twenty-four months is concrete: whether the Aug 2026 transparency obligations are enforced against a named deployer (ledger claim trust-2026-07-02), whether Dec 2027 survives (trust-2026-07-01), whether any state law survives DOJ challenge intact (authority-2026-07-01/02), and whether at least three more jurisdictions adopt "simplification" deferrals (governance-2026-07-03). The deeper long-term stake is constitutional in the broad sense: societies are deciding, mostly by default, whether the governance of the most consequential technology of the era is a public function. The 0–5 year window is when that default gets set, because private ordering, once load-bearing, is politically expensive to displace.
