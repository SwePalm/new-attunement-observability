Turn 1 – Conceptual Frame

Learning has always been governed by permission as much as by instruction. A teacher decides what counts as the student's own work, which tools may be on the desk during a test, whether a calculator is a crutch or an instrument. For most of the history of schooling those permissions were set close to the work: in the room, by the person who would grade it, and they changed slowly enough that a student could learn them once a year. The question the present moment raises is not whether generative AI helps or harms learning in general. It is where the permission to use it now lives, and what happens to learning when that permission is set in several places at once and those places disagree.

The evidence from autumn 2026 makes the layering concrete. New York City suspended student-facing generative AI for grades 2-K to 8 for one school year, and implemented the suspension not by blocking platforms but by switching off AI components inside 38 existing contracts. Los Angeles Unified restricted student use on school-issued devices across all grades for the same year. In the same weeks Google made Gemini in Classroom available by default to students of every age, so that any district that had not already built a separate administrative unit for minors had, without deciding anything, decided yes. Florida adopted two rules on one day: a K-12 rule organised around parental notice, opt-out and a non-AI alternative, and a college rule that bars AI on graded work unless the instructor permits it.

These are not four positions on a spectrum. They are four different answers to a prior question: who decides. The city answers with an executive moratorium; the vendor answers with a default; the state answers with a parent's signature in one sector and an instructor's syllabus line in the other. Learning, understood as the slow construction of a capability the student owns, sits beneath all four and is addressed by none of them directly. The rules speak about access, safety, integrity and consent. They rarely speak about what the student is supposed to be able to do at the end of the term, and when they do, the evidence they could cite is thinner than the confidence with which they are written.

Turn 2 – Societal Reframing

The public argument about AI in schools has been framed as a contest between enthusiasm and caution, and the autumn of 2026 appears at first to be caution winning. That reading misses the more important shape. Caution and enthusiasm won at the same time, in different sectors, often in the same families. In the fortnight that New York and Los Angeles removed student-facing tools, the University of Sydney and UNSW committed to ChatGPT Edu for all of their students, with student access at Sydney beginning in Semester 1 2027. A household with a twelve-year-old in a restricted district and a nineteen-year-old at a partner university now lives under two opposite institutional theories of what the technology is for.

The reframing that follows is that the relevant social unit is no longer the school but the student's week. A single young person may pass through a classroom where AI is switched off at the contract layer, a college course where it is permitted by one instructor and not by the next, a personal phone where it is always on, and a kitchen table where the family decides. The rules do not compose; they stack. What a student learns first, before any subject content, is how to read which rule applies where, and how to behave when the rules conflict. That is a real skill, but it is a skill of compliance and navigation rather than of the subject being taught.

The politics are also changing register. In Chicago, 24 of 45 candidates for the first fully elected board signed a pledge for a three-year moratorium before a single vote was cast, and the district shelved plans to give high school students Gemini. AI in the classroom has become a ballot position, which means it will now move on electoral time rather than on evidence time. South Korea offers the other half of the picture: its AI digital textbooks, downgraded to optional material in 2025, were used by 5.2 percent of schools in the first semester of 2026, down from 34.2 percent a year earlier. Mandates can be reversed as quickly as they are imposed, and the students inside them experience each reversal as a change in what their work means.

Turn 3 – Psychological Implications

For a student, the most consequential psychological fact about the current rules may be their instability rather than their content. A rule that says "no AI on graded work" is a clear constraint; a rule that holds this term, was different last term, and depends on which teacher, which device and which signature is on file produces a different state of mind. Learning requires a period of being stuck: the stretch between not understanding and understanding, in which the student has to tolerate their own confusion long enough for it to reorganise into competence. That stretch is uncomfortable, and it is easiest to endure when its terms are predictable and when someone the student trusts treats it as normal.

Shifting permissions change the meaning of being stuck. Where AI is prohibited, struggle is compulsory and, because it happens under suspicion of cheating, it is often solitary. Where AI is permitted, struggle becomes optional, and a student who chooses it is choosing the slower path while classmates choose the faster one. Where the permission is unclear, struggle acquires a third meaning: it becomes a risk calculation. The Khanmigo trial published as an NBER working paper in August 2026 hints at how students actually behave when the tool is simply available: 96 percent tried the tutor, but the median student messaged it on only a third of practice days, and the gains resembled those of practice without AI. Availability did not transform effort; it redistributed it.

For parents, the psychological load is different and less discussed. Florida's K-12 rule gives parents notice and the right to opt their child out, with a non-AI alternative. That is a real protection, and it is also a transfer of judgement to people who, in most households, have not used the tools in an educational setting and cannot evaluate them. A parent who opts out may be protecting the child or isolating them; a parent who does not may be consenting to something they do not understand. The signature carries the weight of a decision without the information that would make it one. Stanford's SCALE brief, which found strong evidence for AI tutoring only where humans remain central, suggests the parent's presence may matter more than the parent's form.

Turn 4 – Institutional Dynamics

Institutions are reaching for the layer they control. City halls control executive moratoria; districts control contracts and device configurations; state boards control rules and deadlines; vendors control defaults; unions control, or contest, the terms of procurement. The autumn of 2026 shows each of these levers being pulled in isolation. New York acted at the contract layer, disabling features in 38 approved products and allowing five tools into high school pilots. Google acted at the default layer, and districts learned of the change from news coverage. Florida acted at the rule layer, with district policies due by 1 Jul 2027. At Sydney, the National Tertiary Education Union struck on 2 Sep 2026 partly over AI safeguards, while the university argued that AI should be governed by updatable policy rather than the three-year enterprise agreement, and the union and student representatives said they were not consulted before the OpenAI deal was signed.

What this produces is not a hierarchy but a set of overlapping jurisdictions with no rule of precedence. When a dual-enrolled high school student takes a college course on a high school campus, it is not obvious whether the district's K-12 opt-out or the college's instructor override governs the assignment. When a district switches off a vendor feature at the contract layer but the vendor's consumer product remains on the student's own phone, the rule governs the device, not the student. When a one-year moratorium sunsets, the default after the sunset is whatever the vendor and the contract say, unless someone acts again.

The institutions that move first are rarely the ones formally responsible. Florida's college rule came from a state board, not a university system, and its stated rationale was protection and integrity rather than research on cognitive offloading. The phenomenon of restricting AI on graded work arrived through an actor nobody had watched. Deadlines slip, as Maryland's uncertain 120-day window shows, where two sources disagreed on whether the compliance date fell in September or on 22 Oct 2026. The effective rule, in practice, is often the last one written closest to the student: the syllabus line, the teacher's verbal instruction, the family's own decision about the home laptop.

Turn 5 – Long-Term Trajectory

Over the next two to five years the most likely trajectory is not convergence on a single policy but the formalisation of layering itself. One-year moratoria will face renewal decisions, and the likeliest outcome for a large district is partial renewal: restrictions retained for younger grades, pilots expanded in high school, and a committee report that defers the harder questions, as Los Angeles has already signalled by assigning recommendations for later years to a committee. Florida's first school year under district policies written to its amended rule, 2027-28, will be the first large test of whether parental opt-out with a non-AI alternative is administrable at scale, and the likely answer is that it is administrable on paper and uneven in classrooms.

Higher education will keep moving toward institution-wide access, driven by vendor partnerships and procurement cycles, while its assessment practices move toward secured, in-person formats. The two trends are compatible: access for learning, prohibition for grading. That split may prove to be the durable settlement, with the practical consequence that students learn with AI and are assessed without it, and have to maintain two modes of working that do not always transfer. Capital will follow the gap: exam-preparation tutors such as Medly AI, which reported more than 400,000 UK users when it raised 8 million dollars in August 2026, sell precisely to the space between school rules and home practice.

The evidence base will lag the policy. The Eedi and Google DeepMind trial has not published results, and the largest production-tutor trial remains a working paper. Until peer-reviewed outcome evidence arrives, rules will be justified by protection, integrity and cost rather than by learning gains, and they will change when elections, contracts and defaults change. The long-term risk is not that students learn less, which the current evidence does not show, but that learning becomes a set of permissions to be navigated rather than a capacity to be built, and that the adults most able to help a student through the stuck stretch of learning, teachers and parents, are the ones with the least stable authority over the conditions in which it happens.
