Turn 1 – Conceptual Frame

Perception used to be treated as the end of a pipeline: an image or a sentence arrives, a person looks at it, and the question of whether it is synthetic is a question about the object in front of them. The disclosure regimes taking shape in 2026 keep that frame in their vocabulary and abandon it in their mechanics. A label is a sentence addressed to a viewer. A watermark is a sentence addressed to a detector. A reach limit is not a sentence at all; it is a decision about which viewers the object reaches in the first place. These are three different theories of what it means to help someone see, and they are now operating at once, on the same accounts, in the same week.

The clearest example of the third theory arrived at the end of August 2026. Instagram's new "AI-generated profile" badge is a label, but the consequence attached to it is not. An undisclosed synthetic profile is withheld from Reels, Explore and suggestions to people who do not already follow it. Its existing followers keep seeing it, without a badge, until someone adds one. So the rule does not change what a given viewer is told about a given post. It changes which viewers encounter the post at all, and it sorts them by a fact about their past behaviour: whether they followed.

The second theory, the mark addressed to a detector, became real for text on 1 Sep 2026 when two new Claude models began watermarking their outputs and a detection interface opened in private preview for regulators, media, fact-checkers and researchers. The viewer is not on that list. The mark is present in what the viewer reads and is, by design, invisible to them. What it offers is a later reading by someone else.

The first theory, the visible label, is where the law is going. Korea's ministry casebook says a hidden watermark alone is not enough for a deepfake; the mark has to be visible for the whole of playback. That is a claim about the viewer's eyes, not about a file.

The conceptual problem, then, is that "perception of synthetic media" has split into three objects: what a viewer is told, what a machine can later establish, and what a viewer was never shown. Only the first is experienced. The other two shape experience without appearing in it. A person can perceive a label. Nobody perceives a withholding. The perception theme in this horizon is less about whether people can tell real from synthetic and more about whether the conditions of seeing are themselves perceptible.

Turn 2 – Societal Reframing

At the scale of a society, labels were supposed to be a common reference: a shared marker that means the same thing to everyone who sees it, so that disagreements about content could at least start from agreement about its origin. The 2026 record points toward something else, which is disclosure that partitions audiences rather than informing them.

Consider the same account across a household. One person followed a pleasant synthetic persona in 2025 and still sees its posts in their feed, unbadged, because the creator never disclosed and detection of unlabeled profiles was promised for "the coming weeks" rather than shipped. Another person, who never followed, does not see the account at all; the reach limit keeps it out of their discovery surfaces. A third, on the sibling app where the rule has not been extended, sees it in suggestions as ordinary content. None of these people is misinformed in the sense a label is designed to address. Each is simply looking at a different world, and the difference is produced by an enforcement choice that aims at the creator's incentive rather than the viewer's understanding.

This is rational from the platform's side. Reach is the currency that creators respond to, so restricting reach is the lever with the fastest effect on disclosure rates. But the social result is that the label becomes a mark carried by the accounts that told the truth, while the accounts that did not are simultaneously hidden from new audiences and preserved, undisclosed, for loyal ones. The badge concentrates on the honest; the silence concentrates on the already-attached.

Meanwhile the stripping response shows the other half of the social picture. Within about a week of Claude's text watermark being announced, searches for watermark removers rose 60% and a remover project passed 15,000 stars. Korea mandates marks at generation and has no current provision punishing their removal, and its own help desk logged 513 inquiries in two months, more than half about how to label at all. The effect is a disclosure layer populated by the conscientious and the confused, not by those most motivated to deceive.

The societal reframing is therefore that disclosure is becoming a sorting system rather than a signal: it sorts creators by honesty, viewers by prior attachment, and jurisdictions by how visible they insist the mark must be. Shared perception, the thing labels were meant to protect, is what the sorting erodes.

Turn 3 – Psychological Implications

People learn to read cues faster than institutions learn to issue them. A badge that appears mostly on accounts that chose to disclose will be learned, within months, as information about the creator rather than about the content. "This account is labeled" comes to mean "this account tells you things", and an unlabeled account comes to feel neutral, which is to say human. The psychological risk is not that people disbelieve labels. It is that they over-believe the absence of one.

There is a second, quieter effect in the experience of a label arriving late. When a persona someone has followed for a year acquires a badge, nothing about the content changes, but the relationship does. The viewer is told, retroactively, that a year of small attentions were directed at them by something that was not what they took it to be. The anger that follows lands on the account that disclosed, because disclosure is the visible event. The accounts that never disclose never provide the moment of betrayal, and so are never punished for it by the people who follow them.

Third, a mark that is invisible to the viewer and legible to someone else produces a specific feeling, which is being watched on one's own behalf. A person talking to a bank's video avatar in 2027 may be told that the avatar is watermarked, as every Gemini Live Avatar is with SynthID, and may reasonably wonder whom that protects. The honest answer is that it protects a later reader: an auditor, a court, a fact-checker. It gives the person in the conversation nothing to perceive. Protections that are real but not experienced tend to be discounted, and then resented when they create friction.

Fourth, perception becomes a setting. TikTok already lets users slide the amount of AI-generated content they are shown, a control that predates this theme's claims. Combined with reach limits, the experience of the synthetic becomes partly chosen and partly assigned, and people lose the ability to say whether what they are not seeing is absent from the world or only absent from them. The psychological cost of that is not paranoia; it is a mild, persistent uncertainty about whether one's own sense of how much synthetic content exists is a fact about the world or about one's feed.

Finally there is the instrument's own effect on what it marks. Lasso's September study reports that the SynthID-Text scheme, tested on open-weight models, shifts refusal behaviour and tool-call arguments. People who learn this will reasonably ask whether a marked answer is the same answer they would otherwise have received, and there is no way for them to check.

Turn 4 – Institutional Dynamics

Four kinds of institution are acting on perception, and they move at very different speeds.

Platforms move fastest, and they move through distribution. Instagram's rule was announced and live on the same day, and its lever was reach. Detection of undisclosed profiles, which would give the rule force against those who ignore it, was promised rather than delivered, and the rule's scope was Instagram only. Whether it extends to Facebook or Threads is an open, checkable question. Platforms' institutional logic is to use the incentive they control and to avoid the one they would have to defend in public: a viewer-facing interstitial on content a creator chose to post.

Model vendors move next, through defaults and gated access. Anthropic's marking reached pre-cutoff models within weeks, and a detection interface opened for approved parties. Apple, working at the device layer, slipped: SynthID was announced for iOS 27 and did not ship at general release, deferred to a later update. Vendor timing is therefore uneven by layer, with model-side changes faster than operating-system integration. These are private orderings with public effects, and they are moving well ahead of statute.

Legislatures and regulators move slowest, and they move toward visibility. Korea's casebook requires a visible mark on deepfakes, its National Assembly subcommittee held tampering sanctions and platform preservation duties for further review on 15 Sep 2026, and its grace period on fines runs to 22 Jan 2027. The EU's Article 50(2) marking date of 2 Dec 2026 stood at the AI Board's September plenary, but seven weeks into enforceability there was no named Article 50 action. California's AI Transparency Act became operative on 2 Aug 2026. New York's Stop Deepfakes Act passed both chambers in June and had not reached the Governor by late September. Oregon's investigation into unlabeled AI campaign videos had no determination. The statutory clocks are real, but their visible effects lag the platform layer by months or years.

The fourth institution is the detection market, which sells to organisations rather than to viewers. KPMG's minority stake in Reality Defender in September 2026 folds deepfake detection into fraud and cyber services for enterprise clients. Detection is being institutionalised as a back-office function of banks and insurers, not as something an individual consults before believing a video.

The dynamic that follows is a mismatch of addressees. Statute increasingly addresses the viewer's eyes; platforms address the creator's reach; vendors address an approved reader; the detection market addresses the enterprise. No institution in the current record addresses what the viewer was not shown.

Turn 5 – Long-Term Trajectory

Over the next two to four years the most likely trajectory is not a single labeling regime but an uneven layering that makes perception more personal and less shared.

Visible labels will spread, pushed by statute where it insists on visibility and by platform badges where reach is at stake. They will remain heavily dependent on self-disclosure, because detection of unlabeled synthetic content remains promised more often than shipped, and because the people most motivated to avoid a label are the ones most willing to strip a mark. The label will therefore increasingly carry information about who chose to disclose, and viewers will learn to read it that way.

Distribution penalties will become the main enforcement currency, because they are cheap to operate, fast to change and do not require a platform to take a public position on any specific post. If Meta extends its reach limit to Facebook or Threads, and others follow with comparable rules, the practical meaning of "undisclosed synthetic content" will become "content that new audiences are not shown", and existing audiences will be left with what they already chose. The pattern will reward loyalty with less information, not more.

Invisible marks will keep spreading at the generation layer, reaching more models and eventually operating systems, and their readers will remain gated for some time. The Korean route, sanctions on removal and preservation duties on platforms, is the main counter-pressure, and it depends on a legislative calendar that had just deferred the question to an October session. The EU's December date will arrive, but the first named enforcement action is likely to lag it by a long interval.

A second-order effect is worth naming. Marks may alter outputs. If the evidence that watermarking shifts refusals and tool arguments holds up and is not answered by the vendors, a small but persistent line of argument will emerge that marked output is a different output, and that disclosure has a cost to the thing disclosed. That will not stop marking, but it will slow the case for extending it to agentic use.

The long-term question for perception is whether any institution takes responsibility for the shared view: the assurance that two people looking at the same account are being shown the same account with the same words around it. Nothing in the current record points to one doing so within five years. What is more likely is that people build that assurance themselves, informally, by comparing screens, and that the comparison becomes an ordinary social habit rather than a public good.
