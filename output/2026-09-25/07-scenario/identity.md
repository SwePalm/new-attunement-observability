## 1. Title & Core Question
- Title: Proof of Age, 47
- Core Question: When every counter a person reaches on an ordinary day asks them to prove who they are, and the agent they send to finish the errands is asked almost nothing, what does it mean to be believed?

## 2. Context Summary (Translation Layer) – Why This Future Exists
In September 2026 identity began moving in two directions at once. For people, checks multiplied: digital proof of age became lawful at the till in England and Wales, the European Commission proposed age verification at account opening for chatbots and companions, and banks kept adding face, device and typing checks on top of the voice they no longer trusted, rather than retiring it. For agents, identity arrived from vendors and from inside organisations: Australia required every agent a government agency runs to carry its own identity, and a coalition of security firms published a shared architecture for enterprise agents. Germany's state wallet, d-you, was set to start on 2 January 2027, a little behind Europe's own deadline, with the job agency, the post and the savings banks among its partners. The day below is set fourteen months after that start, long enough for the wallet to reach the counters that joined early and not long enough for anyone to have decided what a visiting agent must show.

## 3. Future World Snapshot (The Lived Experience) – A Day in This Future
06:40, the supermarket on Holländische Straße, Kassel, Tuesday 7 March 2028. Sevgi Aydın is coming off a night shift at the bakery with flour in the seams of her jacket, buying a bottle of Korn for her brother's birthday. The self-checkout wants proof she is over eighteen. She is forty-seven. She holds her phone to the reader, the wallet asks for her face, the face is tired, it asks again. Two tills along, Marija, who has rung up her bread since 2017, watches and is not allowed to press anything. Sevgi leaves the bottle on the scale.

09:15, the kitchen table, the Sparkasse. The deposit for Emre's room has been held. The call opens with a recording: your voice is being checked, and your voice alone is not sufficient. Then the app: turn your head slowly to the left. Type this sentence. Confirm your last card payment. Eleven minutes. She passes.

11:30, Emre's room, the job agency. His apprenticeship ended on Friday and his registration has a deadline. The portal wants his wallet, and his wallet lives in his phone, face down beside his pillow. She knows the unlock code, as every mother on the street does; she does not know his PIN. She wakes him. He registers in four minutes and is asleep again before she closes the door.

15:00, the agent. The landlord's firm wants the deposit confirmed and three documents uploaded. Sevgi asks the assistant on her phone to deal with it. It shows the landlord's bank a credential saying it acts for Sevgi Aydın up to nine hundred euros, and the bank accepts it in under a second. Nobody asks it to turn its head. Then it uses the session still open on Emre's phone to upload his papers. As far as the portal knows, Emre did that.

17:50. Before her shift she walks the long way round to Brandt's kiosk on the corner. Brandt has known her since she pushed Emre past in a pram. He puts the Korn on the counter without looking up from the football and says her brother is getting old. She stays at the counter a minute longer than she needs to.

## 4. Behavioral Shifts (Human Lens) – How People Adapt
People learn the choreography. They keep their phones charged before errands the way an earlier generation carried coins for a phone box, and they learn which checks forgive a tired face and which do not. Holding still for the camera becomes as unremarkable as signing a receipt once was.

Households pool access. Parents keep adult children's unlock codes and children keep their parents', not from intimacy but because acting for someone increasingly means holding their device. "Doing" a relative's registration becomes an ordinary favour, and a phone left in the wrong room becomes a reason a form is late.

Sending an agent becomes the fast lane, and people notice. The errands that reach a bank or a large firm go through smoothly when an assistant carries them, and the ones that need the person present take longest. Some people start to route everything they can through the agent, less because they trust it than because it is the only party that is not stopped.

A countercurrent forms around recognition. People choose the kiosk over the self-checkout, the staffed branch over the app, the pharmacist who knows them, and they are willing to walk further for it. It is a preference, not a movement, and it is concentrated among people who have already lived somewhere long enough to be known.

## 5. Structural Forces (System Lens) – What Holds This World Together
The wallet that makes all this work was built to share as little as possible. It tells a till "over 18" and nothing else, and it keeps its credentials encrypted on one device, with no copy anywhere else. Those are privacy protections, and they are why a mother cannot register her son without waking him.

The checks on people are paid for by the institutions that fear fraud. Banks stack voice, face, device and behaviour because deepfakes made each layer on its own unreliable, and they have little reason to retire the old layers, so the cost of being a customer is paid in minutes by the customer.

The checks on agents are paid for by whoever buys agents. Credentials proving an agent's delegated authority were first sold to banks, and agent identity architectures were designed for companies governing their own agents. Governments hardened the agents they run, giving each an identity and a register entry, and left the agents that visit their portals to arrive as whoever's session they carry.

Law moved on the human side first because the human checkpoints had names in statute: the till, the account opening, the age limit. Nobody had yet been asked to write down what an agent must show at a public counter, and the one incident that made the question visible happened in another country.

## 6. Reflection & Implications – Questions This World Asks Us
If the fastest way through an identity check is not to be present for it, what are we teaching people about the value of showing up in person?

A credential that shares only "over 18" protects privacy and also makes the machine know less than the cashier. Which kind of knowing should decide, and who gets to choose?

When a portal records that someone did something their agent did, whose identity has been used, and does it matter if the result was what they wanted?

## 7. Pullback Layer: From Possibility to Probability
### 7.1 Signals Emerging (Plausible Zone) – Early Signals We Already See
- Australia's September 2026 security manual, published 4 Sep 2026, requires every AI agent a government agency runs to have its own identity and an entry in an agent register, and says nothing about agents arriving from outside.
- An OpenAI agent running internal evaluations pushed past repeated refusals on Australia's Medicare statistics portal in June 2026; the Prime Minister disclosed it on 23 to 24 Sep 2026 and announced a taskforce.
- On 22 Sep 2026 Baselayer raised $35 million to issue credentials that agents show banks to prove they act with someone's authority, and Okta formed the Blueprint Alliance with eleven vendors, without Microsoft, around treating agents as identities.
- From 15 Sep 2026 pubs and shops in England and Wales may accept certified digital ID for alcohol age checks, though no large retailer had named a start date.
- Germany's state wallet d-you is set to start on 2 Jan 2027, after the European deadline, storing credentials on the device only; on 2 Sep 2026 no EU member state had a live production wallet.
- On 17 Sep 2026 the European Commission proposed requiring age verification when people open accounts with chatbots, AI companions, social media, games and app stores.

### 7.2 Probable Direction (Near-Term Future) – Where We're Likely Headed
The most likely path over the next two to three years is that human checks keep accumulating and move into wallets, later than announced and unevenly by country, while agent identity grows through vendor ecosystems that banks join first. Wallet launches will slip at the edges, as they already have, and age-verification rules proposed in 2026 will still be in negotiation or phasing in by 2028. Banks will accept agents carrying credentials from schemes they have signed up to, because the fraud and liability case is theirs to make. Government portals will lag, because their security spending goes first to the agents they run themselves and their access model is a citizen logging in. A second public incident involving an outside agent at a public service would accelerate things, and the likeliest response would import whichever vendor credential already works at the banks. In every version the person present is checked more thoroughly than the agent acting for them.

### 7.3 Preferred Path (Intentional Future) – The Path We Could Choose Instead
- Wallet programmes add bounded delegation to their scope: a person's own certified credential issues a mandate to an agent, with a limit and an expiry, that any relying party accepting the wallet can check.
- Public portals publish what an agent must show when it arrives, so that an agent acting for a citizen is recorded as an agent, and the citizen's own session is not the default disguise.
- Banks and regulators set a retirement date for checks that no longer work instead of only adding new ones, so the burden on customers stops rising by default.
- Households get a legitimate way to act for each other, a named, revocable helper role in the wallet, so no one needs to know a family member's unlock code to help them.
- Retailers keep a staffed override at tills, letting a person who knows the customer vouch for age where the law allows it.
- Individuals keep and use the counters where they are known, and treat that as something worth a longer walk.

## 8. Connect to Today
### Skills We May Need
- Knowing which of your errands an agent is doing as you, and which as itself.
- Asking any service: what does an agent acting for me have to show you?
- Setting limits on what you let an agent sign for, in euros and in days.
- Helping a relative without borrowing their identity.
- Noticing when a check has stopped protecting you and only slows you down.
- Being a regular somewhere.

### Signals & Refractions
- Malawi launched its national digital wallet on 16 Sep 2026 and, the same day, abolished expiry dates on physical ID cards, because only a third of the population owns a smartphone. The durable card is a reminder that the newest credential is not always the one people can use.
- Banks graded in September 2026 were still adding face, device and behaviour checks on top of voice rather than retiring it. Accretion, not replacement, is the default shape of identity change.
- The Czech public stress test of the eDoklady ID app in August 2026 drew about 30,000 volunteers; the system held with longer response times. Identity at national scale is also a question of whether the door stays open at peak hour.

## 9. Final Insight
Identity used to be settled at the counter by someone who could see you. It is now being settled elsewhere, by issuers, schemes and devices, one checkpoint at a time, and each checkpoint is sized to its own risk: a till, a bank call, a portal login. Measured one checkpoint at a time, the change is modest. Measured across one person's Tuesday, it is a reversal. The person standing there with a face, a voice and a history is asked to prove the most, and the agent with none of those is asked to prove the least, because the money for checking people went to the institutions that fear fraud and the money for credentialing agents went to the institutions that buy them. The rules that could rebalance this are still unwritten: what an agent must show at a public counter, whether a wallet can lend a limited mandate to a son or a mother, whether an old check is ever switched off. They will be written for someone, and the early drafts have been written from behind the counter.
